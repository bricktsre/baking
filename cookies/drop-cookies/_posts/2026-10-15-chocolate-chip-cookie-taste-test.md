## Intro
As the cold weather sets in and the holiday season approaches, my cookie production naturally ramps up. Every year I try to make cookies a little bit better than the previous year. This cookie season I am focused on upgrading key ingredients to "premium" varieties in various recipes. The first ingredient I am testing is chocolate chips.

Normally I would buy the cheapest chocolate chips available, which is usually store brand. A larger bag () would run $ (/oz) which is very affordable but it also has cacao of unknown quality and ingredients like (). Earlier this year I decided to go slightly nicer with Tollhouse chocolate chips. A strong dislike for Nestle motivated me to find something even better. Three of the largest names in premium chocolate are Valrhona, Callebaut, and Guittard. I would say they are pretty equal in terms of quality and price. I like Guittard's cocoa powder and have heard good things about their chocolate chips so they are the ones I spent the most time researching. It turns out you can get 25lbs of Guittard chocolate chips at a pretty [reasonable price][webstaurantstore-guittard]. Before buying 25lbs of chocolate chips I wanted to figure out which cacao percentage would work best in the normal [chocolate chip cookie recipe][sally-choc-chip] I make.

The three Guittard varieties I chose were: 
- Milk Chocolate Chips (31% cacao)
- Super Cookie Chips (48% cacao)
- Extra Dark Chocolate Chips (63% cacao)

There were three varieties I didn't use since that would have been a lot for a single taste test:
- Semisweet Chocolate Chips (46% cacao), these were very close in cacao to the Super Cookie Chips and those have cookie in the name!
- Akoma Semisweet Chocolate Chips (55% cacao), in a no man's land between semisweet and dark chocolate
- Sante Mini Dark Chocolate Chips (72% cacao), probably too dark for a cookie

In addition to the cacao percentages there were also slight differences in ingredients: cane sugar vs coconut sugar, organic vs non-organic, cocoa butter vs no cocoa butter, and vanilla vs no vanilla. The milk chocolate chips also had milk as the name implies.

## The Test
I split the cookie dough into three batches of about 270g and added 70-80g of chips to each batch. Immediately noticeable was the size and shape difference between the three chocolate chip varieties (hover/click the above images to see). Extra dark were small classic chips and mixed well, milk were large chips and mixed poorly, super cookie were waker-like and mixed very poorly. The two larger ones were not distributed evenly across the size #60 cookie balls I made. 12 cookies, 4 of each batch, were baked at the same time then separated and split in half. 

I brought all the cookie halves into work to gather data from my co-workers. Their only instructions were to have one of each then vote for their favorite. They did not know what was different between the three options. The common refrain I heard was, "All three were actually the same," or "I couldn't taste any difference." Unsurprisingly the votes were even across the board. I had two friends also taste test that knew the only difference was the chocolate. They really took their time and in the end both preferred the super cookie chips. Personally I could tell the milk chocolate from the other two but not really differentiate between Super Cookie and Extra Dark. This was non-blind, self recorded, and small sample size so the results are hardly conclusive.

In the end I am probably going to get 25 lbs of Guittard 41% Semi-Sweet Chocolate 4M baking chips. They are at a good price point and the chips are a good size to mix into cookies or other treats like brownies. Different people would probably prefer different chocolate chips in their cookies. Not to mention that each chocolate chip may do better or worse depending on the recipe. 

In the end buy good chocolate chips you can afford and like the taste of.
